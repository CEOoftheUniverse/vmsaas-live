Why the answer depends on your numbers

In our model, low Earth orbit costs 5.27x Earth at today's launch price of about USD 3,600/kg, and still 1.92x at USD 200/kg. A dedicated Falcon 9 lists at USD 74M in 2026 and lifts 17.5-22.8 t to low orbit (Wikipedia), so about USD 3,250-4,230/kg. Chips are about 65% of an Earth data center's cost and electricity only 5-10%, so free sunlight cannot pay for the launch, the satellite and chips that fail with no repair. Once launch is cheap, satellite build cost and mass move the result more than the launch price does. A memo tests those inputs for your design.

How we work

Each memo uses the same model, sources and checks as our November 2026 space-compute study.

  1. The panel's cost model. A closed-form total-cost-of-ownership (TCO) model, built by our AI research panel, that prices 19 scenarios: six Earth sites, low Earth orbit at five launch prices, Moon-made hardware, Mars orbit and the Mars surface. It counts USD per delivered kW of IT power per year, with 5-year chips, an 8% cost of capital and no margin.
  2. 471 sources. The panel's 58 AI agents read 1,346 web pages and PDFs and kept 471 sources. Every outside number in a memo links to where it came from.
  3. Adversarial fact-checks. A separate AI fact-checking agent attacked each of the 14 main research lanes, looking for second sources, and AI physics, economics and timeline critics then challenged the results. Memos reuse only the study's claims that came out confirmed or corrected, and link a source for anything new.
  4. The calculator. The same formulas run in our free space data center cost calculator. They reproduce the full model within 0.02%, so you can test any number in your memo yourself.
  5. A human physics check. Before delivery, a person checks the memo's power, heat, mass, radiation and orbit numbers.

Break-even launch price

None under central inputs, at any mass. Even free launch and a free satellite only bring orbit to 0.98x Earth. With optimistic hardware (USD 400/kg build cost and 3% a year chip loss), break-even is USD 671/kg at 10 kg/kW and USD 184/kg at 20 kg/kW, and there is none at 33 kg/kW or heavier.

Top 3 risks

  1. Satellite build cost. It moves the result more than any other input, and it is the least-evidenced one: our range runs from USD 400 to 5,000 per kg.
  2. Chips that fail with no repair. At 9% a year, unrepaired chip losses and the 10% space-chip premium cancel the savings on buildings and electricity.
  3. Radiation errors. In Google's proton-beam test, a Trillium TPU had no hard failures up to 15 krad(Si), 20x the dose it would collect behind 10 mm of aluminium over 5 years in low Earth orbit, but its HBM memory showed irregularities from 2 krad. Heavy-ion effects are untested and no in-orbit error data is published yet. Inference tolerates silent data corruption at about 7.5-10 events per chip-year; training must design around it.

Is this investment advice?

No. A memo is an engineering and cost analysis built on the panel's model and public sources. It is not investment, legal or tax advice, and its projections are not guarantees.

Can the memo cover the Moon or Mars?

Yes. The model covers 19 scenarios, from six Earth sites and low Earth orbit at five launch prices to Moon-made hardware, Mars orbit and the Mars surface. At central inputs, Moon-made bulk hardware in low orbit costs 2.17x Earth (1.38x in its low-cost case), and Mars orbit costs 4.51x with all hardware shipped from Earth or 5.56x with Mars-made bulk. Mars sits 3.0-22.3 light-minutes from Earth and can be reached once every 26 months, so it suits Mars-local users and batch jobs.

How reliable are the model's numbers?

The model is closed-form and transparent: every input is sourced or marked as our assumption, and the free calculator runs the same formulas. At BCG's own settings it gives 2.33x Earth, where BCG projects 2.5-3x. Its least-evidenced inputs are satellite build cost and chip losses, so every memo shows how far they move your answer.
