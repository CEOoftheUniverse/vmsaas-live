Radiation-tolerant AI runtime: keep AI correct when bits flip

In a ground proton test, a modern AI chip took 20 times the radiation dose of a 5-year mission in low Earth orbit with no hard failures. The harder problem is bits that flip without a crash. We are building a runtime, planned as open source, to catch those flips and keep training and inference correct. It is in development: size your bit-flip budget below, then request early access.

The problem: chips survive the dose, bits still flip

Radiation harms chips in two ways. Total dose slowly wears them out, and single particles flip bits at random. Google measured both for its Trillium TPU (v6e) in a 67 MeV proton-beam test. The chip showed no hard failures up to 150 Gy (15 krad), 20x the 7.5 Gy (750 rad) it would collect behind 10 mm of aluminium over a 5-year mission in low Earth orbit. Its HBM memory showed irregularities from 20 Gy (2 krad), still about 2.7x that dose. The test used protons only, so heavy-ion effects are still untested, and no in-orbit error data has been published yet.

The open task is silent data corruption: a particle flips a bit and the chip keeps running with a wrong result. The same test saw one silent error per 0.14-0.2 Gy (14.4-20 rad). At about 1.5 Gy (150 rad) a year in low Earth orbit, that is about 7.5-10 silent errors per chip-year, or roughly one every 6 minutes across 10,000 chips. Google's paper puts that at about one bad answer in 3 million at one request per second, so inference tolerates it. Training must design around it, because a wrong value can carry into later steps.

Mars is gentler on total dose but harder on recovery. Heavy ions and storms may raise silent-error rates there, and Earth is 3.0-22.3 light-minutes away, so software must handle resets and storms by itself.

The software layer is still open. NVIDIA has disclosed no single-event mitigation for its Space-1 Vera Rubin module, announced in March 2026 with up to 25x H100 inference compute and no ship date (Payload). Our research found only one dedicated open-source radiation-tolerant ML framework, Space-Radiation-Tolerant, and its results so far come from simulation. Research does back protection in software: RedNet used the uneven sensitivity of neural-network layers to cut the influence of radiation errors to about zero, and sped up on-satellite inference by 8.4-33% at negligible extra cost, in tests with Chaohu-1 satellite data and a radiation emulator.

How the math works

  • Silent errors per day = N × r ÷ 365.25, for N chips at r silent errors per chip-year.
  • Fleet mean time between errors M = 8,766 h ÷ (N × r). A year is 8,766 hours (365.25 days).
  • Checkpoint interval τ = √(2 × C × M), with the checkpoint write time C and M both in seconds (Young, 1974; Daly, 2006).
  • Checkpoint overhead ≈ C ÷ τ + τ ÷ (2M): time spent writing checkpoints, plus half an interval of work redone after each error. At the Young/Daly interval the two parts are equal.
  • Silent errors during the job = N × r × T ÷ 8,766, with the job length T in hours. The chance of at least one is 1 − e^(−that number).
  • Fuller model: run time ÷ error-free run time = M × (e^((τ + C) ÷ M) − 1) ÷ τ, at the τ that makes it smallest (Daly, 2006).

Assumptions: errors arrive at random and independently; every error is caught and sends the whole job back to its last checkpoint, as in one synchronous training run; restarts take no time; all chips share one error rate. The first-order formulas hold when C is much shorter than M. As C nears M, the real cost rises faster, which the fuller model shows.

Why global checkpoints alone fall short at fleet scale

Errors add up with fleet size. Double the chips and the time between errors halves. The best-case checkpoint overhead grows with the square root of the fleet size, so 10 times more chips means about 3.2 times more overhead. With the defaults, 10,000 chips see a silent error about every 7 minutes. A global checkpoint every 3.7 minutes then costs about 53% extra run time (first-order), and the fuller model puts the run at about 1.8 times its error-free length. At 100,000 chips, errors arrive every 42 seconds, faster than a 60-second checkpoint can be written.

Silent errors add a second problem: a checkpoint only helps if you know something went wrong. A silent error leaves no trace, so a plain checkpoint can save the bad state and keep it. Detection has to come first.

That is why our planned runtime will put the checks where errors happen. Algorithm-based fault tolerance (ABFT) checks each matrix multiply as it runs. A mismatch points to the bad value, which can then be fixed or recomputed locally, without rolling back the whole job. Global checkpoints can then become a backstop for rarer events, such as uncorrectable memory errors and host resets, and be spaced further apart.

How a checksum catches a flipped bit

Flipping one bit turns 4 (binary 0100) into 12 (1100). Row 2 and column 2 no longer match their checksums, which were computed alongside the multiply. Together they point to the bad cell, and the gap of 8 gives the fix. For large matrices, the checksums add little work compared with the multiply itself (Huang and Abraham, 1984).

Ground clusters already live with this

Meta's Llama 3 training run on 16,384 H100 GPUs had 419 unexpected interruptions in 54 days, about one every 3 hours, and recovered through checkpoints. At 7.5 silent errors per chip-year, the same fleet in orbit would see a silent error about every 4 minutes (our calculation). The same techniques apply on Earth, where large fleets also see silent data corruption.

Planned v0.1

Fault injection

Flip bits on purpose in weights, activations and memory, at rates you choose. Measure accuracy against the bit-flip rate before you fly. We plan to publish the benchmark with the code.

Planned v0.1

ABFT checksums for matrix multiplies

Checksum rows and columns ride along with each matrix multiply. A flipped value breaks one row sum and one column sum, which locates it and gives the fix, with no global rollback.

Planned v0.1

SEU-aware checkpoint and rollback

A single-event upset (SEU) is a bit flipped by one particle. Set the checkpoint interval from the measured upset rate with the Young/Daly rule, tighten it during solar storms, and roll back only the work an error touched.

Planned

ECC and DCGM telemetry

Export ECC error counts and XID events (the driver's GPU error reports) from NVIDIA's Data Center GPU Manager (DCGM), so you can track error rates per chip, per orbit and during storms.

Planned

Fault-tolerant inference

Check suspect results and re-run them, and add redundancy only to the layers that matter most, the approach RedNet showed can work.

Planned

First targets

NVIDIA GPUs with CUDA, with TensorRT and vLLM support planned. We will build and test on rented cloud GPUs on Earth first.

FAQ

Does radiation break AI chips in orbit?

Not in the tests so far. In Google's proton-beam test, a Trillium TPU had no hard failures up to 150 Gy (15 krad), 20x the dose of a 5-year mission in low Earth orbit. Heavy ions are still untested, and the bigger task is silent bit flips.

What is silent data corruption?

A bit flip that changes a result without a crash or an error message. In low Earth orbit it happens about 7.5-10 times per chip-year, so a 10,000-chip fleet sees one about every 6 minutes. Inference tolerates it. Training needs checks such as ABFT checksums, error-correcting memory and checkpoints.

How often should a GPU fleet in orbit checkpoint?

A common rule is the Young/Daly interval: the square root of 2 × checkpoint time × mean time between errors. For 10,000 chips at 7.5 errors per chip-year and a 60-second checkpoint, that is about every 3.7 minutes, at about 53% extra run time. The calculator on this page works it out for your fleet.

Is the runtime available, and will it be open source?

Not yet. It is in development, and v0.1 is planned as open source, with fault injection, ABFT checksums and SEU-aware checkpoint and rollback. ECC/DCGM telemetry and fault-tolerant inference are planned for a later release. There is no release date yet. Join early access to hear first.

Does it help GPU clusters on Earth too?

It is designed to. Large ground clusters fail often too: Meta's Llama 3 run on 16,384 H100 GPUs had 419 unexpected interruptions in 54 days. The same planned fault injection, checksums, checkpoint tuning and ECC telemetry would apply on Earth.
