Maglev Launch Assist: What an Underground Track Really Buys

November 5, 20268 min readLaunch physics
TL;DR
  • Fuel is cheap: propellant is 0.3-1.6% of a launch price, so the fuel a maglev push saves is worth only USD 39-243k per launch (panel's model).
  • Payload is the prize: an angled 270-300 m/s release adds about 24-30% payload to a Falcon-9-class rocket in the panel's 2-D model (12-21% in simpler estimates).
  • A spring adds almost nothing: a 10 m stroke at 3 g gives 24 m/s, and a Falcon-9-class rocket would need 300-720 t of spring steel for it.
  • Power is the hard part: about 3-6 GW (Falcon-9-class) to 26-53 GW (Starship-class), delivered in about 10 seconds.
  • The Moon is the best home: escape takes 0.79 kWh/kg against 3.5 for Mars. On Earth, the test to watch is China's planned maglev pad (target 2028).

The idea behind maglev launch assist is appealing: an electric track under the pad, like a maglev train in a tunnel, pushes the rocket to speed before its engines light, and a spring platform adds a final kick. Electricity is cheap, so the rocket should need less fuel.

We tested it with our study panel's launch-physics model, a 2-D ascent of a 550 t Falcon-9-class rocket and a 5,000 t Starship-class stack at 3 Nov 2026 fuel prices (model results, not flight data). The physics works, but the value is payload, not fuel, and the best site is the Moon.

How would an underground maglev launch work?

A maglev sled carries the rocket along a launch rail and lets go at a set speed; the engines fly the rest. In the panel's model, shape matters as much as speed:

  • A horizontal underground train is the weakest layout. Turning upward at 270 m/s needs a curve of 2.5-3.7 km radius.
  • A tunnel sloping up at 75-77 degrees works best. It is about 1.2-1.5 km long at 3 g, plus a braking run for the sled.
  • A vertical shaft keeps only about +9.5% payload at 270 m/s for a Falcon-9-class rocket, because a fast rocket cannot pitch over in dense air.

Schematic, not to scale. Figures are from the panel's 2-D model for a Falcon-9-class rocket.

Air pressure sets the top speed. Rockets are built for a peak dynamic pressure (max-Q) of about 30-40 kPa. Staying under 40 kPa caps the release at about 256 m/s at sea level and 297 m/s from a 3 km-high exit; a 270-300 m/s sea-level exit meets 45-55 kPa.

Does maglev launch assist save rocket fuel?

Yes, but the fuel is worth little. A Falcon 9 lists at USD 74M (Payload), and the one public Starship price is a 2024-era USD 90M commitment (Motley Fool). At 3 Nov 2026 spot prices for kerosene jet fuel and natural gas (US EIA), propellant is 0.3-1.0% of the Falcon 9 price and 0.6-1.6% of the Starship price.

So the fuel a push saves is worth only USD 39-243k per launch, and the electricity it uses costs less than USD 9,000.

How much extra payload does it give?

Here the track earns its keep. A head start lets the rocket skip its slow, gravity-heavy first seconds, so each m/s of push is worth about 1.8 m/s of rocket performance in the panel's model (2.0 for the Starship-class stack).

When does a USD 3B track pay for itself?

Fuel cannot repay a track; payload can, if the rocket flies often.

So only a high-cadence launch operator captures the value. The track also has rivals: stretching the rocket by 10-25% buys similar payload with no new infrastructure, and extra payload is worth about 20x less once launch prices fall toward USD 200/kg.

Can a spring launch platform help?

Very little. Spring steel stores only 300-717 J/kg, while a rocket at 300 m/s carries about 45 kJ in every kilogram. A 10 m stroke gives 24 m/s at 3 g whatever the rocket's mass, and a Falcon-9-class rocket would need 300-720 t of spring steel for it.

The useful "spring" is regenerative braking: the sled brakes into flywheels and returns the energy for the next launch.

How much power does an electromagnetic launch assist need?

Energy is cheap; power is hard. Peak power is mass × acceleration × speed. At 2-4 g and 270 m/s that is about 3-6 GW for a ~550 t Falcon-9-class rocket and 26-53 GW for a ~5,000 t Starship-class stack (simple physics, our calculation). At 3 g the push lasts about 10 seconds.

Batteries cannot discharge that fast. The store has to be flywheels, pulsed alternators or supercapacitors, like the flywheel alternators of the EMALS carrier catapult, whose full 484 MJ store would give a Falcon 9 only 42 m/s (the panel's calculation). A push from below at 2-3 g also raises a rocket's base loads about 2-2.7x, so rocket and cradle must be designed together.

Who is building a maglev rocket launch pad now?

China plans the first real-world test. Galactic Energy, with the China Aerospace Science and Industry Corporation (CASIC) and the Ziyang Commercial Space Launch Technology Research Institute, plans a pad whose superconducting magnets push a rocket vertically past Mach 1 before ignition. It is targeted for 2028 (SCMP; Interesting Engineering). It is announced, not built, and no g-level, track length or budget has been published.

Li Ping, the institute's president, says the pad could double payload; that claim has a single source. In the panel's model a release near Mach 1 is worth about +25-60%, less from a strictly vertical exit, and doubling needs 500-1,000 m/s.

Why is the Moon the best place for a mass driver?

Off Earth the idea gets much stronger: the Moon has no air, and orbit or escape needs only 1.7-2.4 km/s. In the panel's model a lunar mass driver gives each kilogram 0.39 kWh of kinetic energy for low-lunar-orbit speed and 0.79 kWh for escape, against 3.5 to escape Mars and 17.4 for Earth. At 50% efficiency and USD 0.08/kWh that is USD 0.06-0.13 of electricity per kilogram.

NASA's 1977 Ames design was sized to fire 10.5 kg payloads at 2.4 km/s. SpaceX, Moon-first since February 2026 (Payload; NBC News), names lunar factories for AI satellites and a lunar mass driver in its IPO filings, with no date (The Next Web).

Two tasks come first. Lunar power must get cheap: at a rough USD 340-1,700/kWh from a first reactor, launch energy alone would cost USD 270-2,700/kg (the panel's estimate from unverified inputs). And launch savings must matter: at USD 500/kg, launch is only about 5% of the cost of deploying an AI satellite (Casey Handmer).

Does launch assist make space data centers cheaper?

Only a little. In the panel's cost model chips are about 65% of a ground data center's cost, so even free launch plus a free satellite only brings orbit to 0.98x a ground site (the full 2026 space data center cost math). Test your own launch price in the space data center cost calculator.

What would change the answer

  • China's pad flies. Real release speeds and payloads would replace these estimates.
  • A rocket built for the track. The gains above are upper bounds; a rocket designed with its cradle would show what survives the sled loads.
  • Cheap pulse power. Gigawatts for 10 seconds at low cost would solve the hardest engineering task.
  • Lunar industry. NASA's first lunar reactor, LR-1, is planned to land in 2030, and its draft procurement asks for at least 20 kWe; a 1,000 t/yr factory needs 5-10 MWe. The panel's median projection for such a factory is 2045-48.

Frequently asked questions

Does a maglev launch assist save rocket fuel?

Yes, but little money. Propellant is only 0.3-1.6% of a launch price, so a 270-300 m/s push saves USD 39-243k of fuel per launch in the panel's model.

How much extra payload can an electromagnetic launch assist add?

About 24-30% for a Falcon-9-class rocket released from an angled ramp at 270-300 m/s (panel's 2-D model). Simpler estimates give 12-21%; a vertical shaft about 9.5%.

Is anyone building a maglev rocket launch pad?

China's Galactic Energy, with CASIC and the Ziyang launch institute, plans a pad that pushes rockets vertically past Mach 1 before ignition, targeted for 2028. It is announced, not built.

Why is the Moon a better place for a mass driver?

It has no air and weak gravity. Escaping the Moon takes 0.79 kWh/kg, against 3.5 for Mars and 17.4 for Earth, so an electric track can do the whole launch.
