Is Energy 5x Better in Space? Solar Panels Yes, Batteries No

November 5, 20268 min readSpace compute
TL;DR
  • Solar panels: yes. A panel in a dawn-dusk Earth orbit collects about 4-8x the energy of a ground panel, and 4.2-4.6x the best desert tracker.
  • Batteries: no. Their energy is set by chemistry, and about 5,500 charge cycles a year in low orbit limit usable depth of discharge to 10-40%.
  • The real battery win is size. That orbit needs about 40x less storage than 24/7 ground solar.
  • Mars orbit gets 586 W/m², 43% of Earth orbit, so even a high Mars orbit beats a good desert by only about 2-2.8x.
  • Cheap sunlight is not cheap compute. Even at USD 30-200/kg launch, orbital solar power costs USD 318-370/MWh in our model (5-year arrays) against USD 80 on the grid, and electricity is only 5-10% of the cost of AI compute on the ground.

"Energy is 5x better in space" is a common line about orbital data centers. It is half right. Solar panels in the right orbit do collect several times more energy. Batteries store no more. And more sunlight does not make AI compute 5x cheaper. Model figures below come from our research panel's cost model (USD 2026, at cost) and are projections.

Where does "energy is 5x better in space" come from?

The claim traces to three sources, and all three are about solar panels.

  • Starcloud, 2024. Its white paper, Why we should train AI in space, written when the company was still called Lumen Orbit, says "a given solar array in space will generate over 5 times the energy as the same array on Earth". The basis is a capacity factor above 95% in orbit against a 24% US median, plus about 40% stronger peak sunlight.
  • Google Research, 2025. In the blog post introducing Project Suncatcher, Google wrote that in the right orbit a solar panel "can be up to 8 times more productive than on earth". Its Suncatcher research paper compares against a panel at mid-latitude.
  • Elon Musk, 2026. After using the figure at Davos, Musk told the Dwarkesh Podcast (5 Feb 2026) that you get "about five times the effectiveness of solar panels in space versus the ground", adding: "it's not five times cheaper, it's 10 times cheaper because you don't need any batteries."

That quote puts "five times" and "batteries" in one breath, the likely origin of the battery version. None of the three says batteries hold more energy in space.

Is solar power better in space?

Yes, in the right orbit. A dawn-dusk sun-synchronous orbit rides the line between day and night, so a panel at 550-800 km sees full sunlight, 1,361 W/m², for 95-97% of the year. A yearly eclipse season of about 80-99 days still brings shadows of up to 18-22 minutes per orbit (PROBA-2 data plus our fact-checker's shadow model). The size of the gain depends on the ground panel you compare against:

So "4-8x" is a fair summary, and "5x" is a reasonable round number. Our panel plans with about 4-6x against good sunny sites.

Do batteries hold more energy in space?

No. A battery's energy per kilogram is set by its chemistry, and a vacuum does not change it. Orbit makes batteries work harder: a satellite in an ordinary low orbit circles Earth about 15 times a day, roughly 5,500 charge cycles a year. To last, its batteries are held to 10-40% depth of discharge, the share of capacity used per cycle (GomSpace datasheet basis). Usable energy per kilogram is lower in orbit, not higher.

The real win is how little storage you need. A data center on 24/7 ground solar needs about 14-16 kWh of storage per kW to get through the night. In a dawn-dusk orbit the worst eclipse at 550 km needs about 0.36 kWh per kW, roughly 8 kg of batteries per kW at 30% depth of discharge and 150 Wh/kg. That is about 40x less (our panel's calculation). Throttling compute through the eclipse season shrinks it further, at a cost of about 4-5% of a year's energy at 550-650 km.

The accurate version of the claim

A panel in a dawn-dusk orbit collects about 4-8x the energy of a ground panel. Its longest shadows last 18-22 minutes, not a whole night, so the satellite needs about 40x less storage than 24/7 ground solar. Each battery stores the same, or less.

How much sunlight does a panel get in Mars orbit?

Much less. Mars orbit averages 586 W/m², 43% of Earth orbit, swinging between 490 and 713 W/m² over the Mars year (NASA Mars fact sheet). A high, eclipse-free dawn-dusk Mars orbit, at 1,000 km or more, still beats a good Earth desert by about 2-2.8x.

On the surface the gain falls below 1x, with long nights and dust storms that block sunlight for weeks (NASA nuclear power brief). The cold, a mean of about 214 K (NASA fact sheet), also cuts battery performance unless cells are heated.

Is space-based solar power cheaper for data centers?

Not yet. Space-based solar power usually means beaming energy down to Earth, the idea behind ESA's SOLARIS programme. An orbital data center uses the power where it is made. The sunlight is free; the power plant is not. Space-grade triple-junction cells cost about USD 250-450 per watt, against USD 0.28-0.39 for ground silicon (University of Surrey review, single source).

Our model counts arrays, power electronics and batteries at 10.8 kg per kW of computing load and USD 1,000/kg to build, plus launch:

At USD 30-200/kg launch, orbital power costs 2-4.6x the grid, depending on array life. Matching the grid needs power hardware near USD 400/kg, 15-year arrays and launch near USD 30/kg. Starcloud's white paper projects about USD 2 per MWh by counting silicon cells at USD 0.03 per watt and one USD 5M launch per 40 MW, spread over 10 years.

Would 5x cheaper power make AI compute 5x cheaper?

No, because on Earth the chips are the bill. In our model a grid-powered hyperscale site costs USD 11,839 per kW of computing a year, about USD 1.62 per GPU-hour at cost. Chips are about 65% of that and electricity only about 7%, roughly 12 cents per GPU-hour. So 5x cheaper electricity would cut Earth compute costs by only about 6%, and free electricity by about 7% (our calculation).

What would change the answer

  • Lighter, cheaper satellites, the biggest levers in our model. With settings modelled on the Starcloud white paper (USD 30/kg launch, about 4 kg per kW, USD 250/kg build, 3% a year chip loss), orbit comes out at 0.88x Earth. Our central case uses 33 kg per kW.
  • A real break-even. Under our central inputs no launch price makes orbit as cheap as Earth. With USD 400/kg build cost and 3% a year chip loss, it breaks even at USD 671/kg launch for 10 kg per kW, or USD 184/kg for 20 kg per kW.
  • Cheaper cells. Silicon could make satellite power systems up to 90% cheaper in the Surrey review's small-satellite cases (projected), at about 2.6x lower radiation tolerance.
  • Longer-lived arrays. Keeping them 15 years roughly halves orbital power cost, to USD 163-190/MWh at USD 30-200/kg launch.
  • Better ground storage. This one helps the ground: in June 2025 Ember's analysis priced 97% round-the-clock solar in Las Vegas 22% below a year earlier.

Frequently asked questions

Is solar power really 5x better in space?

For solar panels, roughly yes. A panel in a dawn-dusk Earth orbit collects about 4-8x the yearly energy of a ground panel: about 4.2-4.6x the best desert tracker and up to 8x a panel at mid-latitude.

Do batteries store more energy in space?

No. Battery energy is set by chemistry, and in low orbit about 5,500 charge cycles a year limit usable depth of discharge to 10-40%. The gain is that a dawn-dusk orbit needs about 40x less storage than 24/7 solar on the ground.

Is space-based solar power cheaper for data centers?

Not yet. In our model, orbital solar power costs USD 318-370 per MWh with 5-year arrays even at USD 30-200/kg launch, against USD 80 on the grid. Electricity is also only 5-10% of the cost of AI compute on the ground, so cheaper power barely moves the total.

How much sunlight does a solar panel get in Mars orbit?

About 586 W/m² on average, 43% of Earth orbit. A high, eclipse-free Mars orbit still gives about 2-2.8x a good Earth desert, while the Mars surface gives less than a good Earth site.
