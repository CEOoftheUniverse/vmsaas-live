Space data center cost calculator: Earth vs orbit vs Moon vs Mars

Updated 5 Nov 2026Free, runs in your browserModel results, not price quotes

Short answer: in the cost model built by our AI research panel for our November 2026 study, AI compute in low Earth orbit costs about 5.3x as much as on Earth at today's launch price (USD 62,344 vs USD 11,839 per kW of hardware per year) and still about 1.8x at USD 100/kg. Chips are about 65% of the Earth cost and electricity only 5-10%, so free sunlight cannot pay for the launch, the satellite and chips that fail with no repair. Moon-made parts give about 2.2x Earth in the central case. That is more than launching everything from Earth at the USD 100/kg the panel expects by then (1.8x); only a low-cost lunar base (1.4x) does better. Mars orbit costs about 5.6x and suits Mars-local users and batch jobs. Change the inputs below to test your own case.

How the model works

The panel is MoltBot Cloud's AI research panel: AI agents that gathered the sources, built this model and fact-checked its claims. The model is fully open below, so you can check every step.

The calculator runs the closed-form version of the panel's cost model. It reproduces every Earth and Earth-orbit row of the model's scenario table to the dollar, for example USD 11,839 per kW-year on Earth and USD 62,344 in low Earth orbit at today's launch price. Each site's cost is built in seven steps.

  1. Spread upfront costs over the hardware's life. Chips, buildings, satellites and launches are paid up front. Each becomes an equal yearly payment at the cost of capital (8% a year) over the chip life (5 years). An Earth building is spread over 20 years.
  2. Charge interest while you wait. Money spent before the hardware runs costs interest: 3 months for chips on Earth, 9 months on average for the building (an 18-month build, paid as it goes), 6 months to launch a satellite, 9 months to assemble with Moon-made parts and 1.75 years for Earth parts to reach Mars orbit.
  3. Add running costs. On Earth: electricity (price per MWh x PUE x 8.766 MWh per kW-year, about USD 842 at central inputs) plus 3% of the building cost and 3% of the chip cost each year for operations and repairs. In space: operations and insurance at 2.5% a year of everything bought and launched, plus a shared Earth-Mars laser relay in the Mars case.
  4. Divide by the hardware that works. Earth repairs failed chips and is 99.9% available. In orbit nothing is repaired: with 9% of chips lost a year, about 82% are working on average over 5 years (weighted by present value), and the satellite is 99% available. Mars orbit is 97% available.
  5. Make part of the satellite locally. In the Moon and Mars cases, 40% of the satellite's mass (servers, batteries, power electronics, avionics) still comes from Earth. The rest is made locally, weighs twice as much as flight hardware for the same job, and costs the Moon-made or Mars-made price per kg delivered to orbit. Mars orbit gets 586 W/m² of sunlight, 43% of Earth orbit (NASA Mars fact sheet), so its solar arrays are 2.32x larger.
  6. Convert the units. USD per MWh = USD per kW-year ÷ 8.766. USD per GPU-hour = USD per kW-year x 1.2 kW ÷ 8,766 hours.
  7. Solve for break-even. The break-even launch price is the price at which Earth orbit costs the same as the Earth site. If it comes out below zero, no launch price works. The floor is the orbit cost with a free launch and a free satellite.

The cost split follows the panel's model. In space, energy means the solar arrays, power electronics and batteries; build is the rest of the satellite, or the building on Earth; transport is launch and shipping, including the Moon's mass driver and the climb from the Mars surface to orbit. The transport share of a Moon-made or Mars-made price follows the panel's three factory cases. The calculator uses whole dollars per kg for the Moon-made (USD 828) and Mars-made (USD 1,939) prices, so it shows USD 25,729 and 65,832 per kW-year where the model's table shows 25,726 and 65,835. For the same reason the Moon and Mars starting points and the Load links for S8-low, S8-high, S9-low and S9-high land within USD 7 per kW-year (USD 0.01 per GPU-hour) of their table rows.

Show the exact formulas
r = cost of capital, L = hardware life, g = 1 + r CRF(r, n) = r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n) S = average share of chips still working = [ρ ÷ (ρ + f)] × [1 − e^−(ρ+f)L] ÷ [1 − e^−ρL], with ρ = ln(1 + r) and f = chips lost per year Earth = [chips × g^0.25 × CRF(r, L) + building × g^0.75 × CRF(r, 20) + 0.03 × (building + chips)] ÷ 0.999 + electricity × PUE × 8.766 Earth orbit: chips' = chips × (1 + premium); bus = (1 − 0.2446) × mass × build cost; launch = mass × launch price Earth orbit = [(chips' + bus + launch) × (g^0.5 × CRF(r, L) + ops)] ÷ (0.99 × S) Moon: Earth mass = 40% × mass, of which 0.2446 × mass is servers; local mass = 60% × mass × 2 Moon-made = [(chips' + Earth non-server mass × build cost + Earth mass × era launch price) × g^0.75 × CRF(r, L) + local mass × Moon price × g^0.5 × CRF(r, L) + ops × all of it] ÷ (0.99 × S) Mars: flight mass = mass × (1 + 0.235 × 1.32); local mass = (flight mass − Earth mass) × 2; Earth parts ship at 15 × era launch price, with g^1.75 for the wait Mars-made = [Earth parts × g^1.75 × CRF(r, L) + local mass × Mars price × g^0.5 × CRF(r, L) + ops × all of it + relay] ÷ (0.97 × S) Relay = (USD 5B × CRF(r, 15) + USD 0.3B a year) ÷ 1,000,000 kW Break-even launch price = (Earth × 0.99 × S ÷ K − chips' − bus) ÷ mass, with K = g^0.5 × CRF(r, L) + ops Floor = chips' × K ÷ (0.99 × S)

What would change the answer

  • Once launch is cheap, cheaper satellites matter most. At today's USD 3,600/kg, launch is 61% of the orbit cost. At USD 200/kg, spacecraft build cost is the biggest lever in the panel's model: moving it from USD 400 to USD 5,000 per kg moves orbit from USD 17,487 to USD 57,937 per kW-year.
  • Lighter satellites. Mass is the second lever: from 13 to 73 kg per kW, the same case moves from USD 16,023 to USD 36,244.
  • Cheap satellites with chips that last. With USD 400/kg build cost, 3% of chips lost a year, no space-chip premium and 1.5% a year for operations (the Optimistic hardware starting point), orbit breaks even at a launch price of USD 671/kg at 10 kg per kW and USD 184/kg at 20 kg per kW. At 33 kg per kW or heavier there is no break-even.
  • Launch price alone cannot do it. At central inputs no launch price works, because chips are about 65% of the Earth cost and orbit loses them with no repair. Even a free launch plus a free satellite leaves orbit at 0.98x a ground site.
  • What a study counts. Published break-even launch prices span about 10x, USD 50-500/kg: Bain USD 50-100, Forethought about USD 100, McCalip USD 77-260, Google USD 200 or less on energy cost only, and Starcloud about USD 500 for power at about USD 0.05 per kWh (a company claim). The spread comes from what each one counts: the whole data center, the infrastructure, or energy alone.
  • Moon factories. Moon-made bulk beats launching everything from Earth only while Earth launch costs more than about USD 700/kg in the panel's model. A lunar factory needs cheap launch to be built, and at the USD 100/kg the panel expects by then, launching everything from Earth (1.82x Earth) beats central-case Moon-made bulk (2.17x). Only a low-cost lunar base (1.38x) does better, and even free Moon-made hardware leaves orbit at 1.20x Earth.
  • Mars factories. Shipping everything from Earth to Mars orbit (4.51x Earth) stays cheaper than Mars-made bulk (5.56x) until Earth-to-Mars-orbit freight tops about USD 2,550/kg. At SpaceX's advertised USD 100,000/kg Mars cargo price, Mars compute would cost 146x Earth. Even free Mars-made hardware leaves 2.02x, because chips still come from Earth, arrive about 1.75 years late and fail with no repair.

Questions people ask

Are data centers in space cheaper than on Earth?

Not under the panel's central inputs. A data center in low Earth orbit costs about 5.3x a ground site at today's USD 3,600/kg launch price (USD 62,344 vs USD 11,839 per kW-year) and still about 1.8x at USD 100/kg. Chips are about 65% of the Earth cost and electricity only 5-10%, so free sunlight cannot pay for the launch, the satellite and chips that fail with no repair. Even a free launch plus a free satellite only brings orbit to 0.98x a ground site.

What launch price would make orbital AI compute break even?

With the panel's central inputs, none. With optimistic hardware, break-even is USD 671/kg at 10 kg per kW and USD 184/kg at 20 kg per kW, with none at 33 kg per kW or heavier. Optimistic here means four changes: USD 400/kg to build, 3% of chips lost a year, no space-chip premium and 1.5% a year for operations, as in the calculator's Optimistic hardware starting point. Published break-even prices span about 10x, USD 50-500/kg: Bain USD 50-100, Forethought about USD 100, McCalip USD 77-260, Google USD 200 or less on energy cost only, and Starcloud about USD 500 on power cost (a company claim). Falcon 9 costs about USD 3,250-4,230/kg in 2026, and the only public Starship price, a 2024-era USD 90M commitment, is about USD 600-900/kg at 100-150 t.

Isn't solar power 5x better in space?

For solar panels, roughly yes. A panel in a dawn-dusk Earth orbit collects about 4-8x the energy of a ground panel (Google says up to 8x), so the satellite needs about 40x less battery storage than 24/7 ground solar. Batteries store the same or less in space, though, and the hardware is costly to launch: in the panel's model orbital solar delivers power at USD 318-370/MWh with 5-year arrays (USD 163-190 with 15-year arrays) even at USD 30-200/kg launch, against USD 80 from the grid. Electricity is only 5-10% of an Earth data center's cost, so cheaper energy cannot close the gap.

Would factories on the Moon or Mars make it cheaper?

Only in the low-cost cases, and never cheaper than Earth in the panel's model. By the time a lunar factory exists, the panel expects launch near USD 100/kg. At that price, launching everything from Earth gives 1.82x Earth, while central-case Moon-made bulk gives 2.17x: Moon-made bulk only wins while Earth launch costs more than about USD 700/kg. A low-cost lunar base gives 1.38x, and free Moon-made hardware 1.20x. The panel's median date for a lunar factory making 1,000 t a year is 2045-48, and 2038 at the earliest. For Mars orbit, shipping everything from Earth (4.51x) beats central-case Mars-made bulk (5.56x), and even free Mars-made hardware leaves 2.02x, because chips must come from Earth, arrive about 1.75 years late and fail with no repair. Mars orbit sits 3.0-22.3 light-minutes from Earth, so it suits Mars-local users and batch jobs, not interactive use from Earth.

Will radiation ruin AI chips in orbit?

It looks like a solvable task. In Google's proton-beam test, a Trillium TPU (v6e) showed no hard failures up to 15 krad(Si), 20x the dose it would collect behind 10 mm of aluminium over a 5-year low-Earth-orbit mission, though its HBM memory showed irregularities from 2 krad. Heavy-ion effects are still untested and no in-orbit error data has been published yet. The open task is silent data corruption at about 7.5-10 events per chip-year (the panel's estimate from Google's test): inference tolerates it, while training needs error-correcting memory, frequent checkpoints and redundant runs. The calculator's 9% a year chip loss is a hardware failure rate with no repair, not a radiation figure.
